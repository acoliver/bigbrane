# Before the transformer: language, learning, and representations

Research brief for Andrew Oliver, October 2026. Part 1 of 2.

## Conclusion

Earlier research supplies mathematical tools for compositional meaning, reusable concepts, and bounded-state computation. It supplies no demonstrated system that simultaneously matches general-purpose LLM capabilities, learns from child-scale language exposure, and avoids growing compute or memory costs. The most relevant candidates are probabilistic program induction for few-shot generalization, vector-symbolic binding for compact structured representations, and compositional semantics for specifying what a meaning-preserving operation should do. Their bottlenecks differ: hypothesis search, interference, and learning the right structures and operators.

An architectural investigation should separate three questions: how ideas are represented, how those representations are acquired, and how inference uses them. A compact representation can be expensive to discover. A linear-time sequence model can require much training data. A one-shot learner can inherit substantial knowledge through its prior.

## Reading and evidence conventions

Numbered citations refer to the reference list. Links and bibliographic identities are checked by live search or fetch; quantitative claims are included when accessible source text supports them. A historical explanation of why an approach lost adoption is a synthesis unless a source makes that comparison directly. These lineages overlap and several remain active. “Overtaken” below means reduced dominance in broad-coverage language engineering, rather than a mathematical refutation.

Complexities use sequence length n, representation width d, label count K, and memory-slot count M. Fixed-width recurrence and dense attention have different dependence on n; increasing d, M, training examples, or search steps can erase that advantage. This brief does not equate text prediction with all of meaning or cognition.

## 1. Formal grammar and symbolic NLP

**Core idea and formalism.** A finite set of rules can generate indefinitely many structured expressions. Compositional semantics assigns meanings to their parts and combines them according to syntax: a grammar may use `S → NP VP`, while semantic function application gives `⟦VP(NP)⟧ = ⟦VP⟧(⟦NP⟧)`.

**Landmarks.** Noam Chomsky's *Syntactic Structures* (1957) formalized phrase structure and transformations [1]. Richard Montague's *Universal Grammar* (1970) treated natural-language interpretation with mathematical methods related to formal languages and intensional logic [2]. Chomsky's later poverty-of-the-stimulus argument asks which constraints a learner must contribute when its experience underdetermines a grammar. Do not date the entire innateness argument to the 1957 book: Pullum and Scholz's historical and empirical assessment (2002) distinguishes these developments and challenges claims that decisive evidence is absent [3]. Rule-based parsers, semantic interpreters, and knowledge-based expert systems operationalized overlapping pieces of this program; SHRDLU provides a concrete example in section 11.

**Achievements.** Recursion, constituent structure, quantifier scope, and relations between sentences became explicit objects of analysis. Montague supplied a route from syntactic combination to truth-conditional interpretation. These are explanatory and representational achievements; the cited foundational works do not report modern held-out NLP benchmark scores.

William Woods's augmented transition network parser (1970) used grammar arcs with conditions and structure-building actions, including copying and reordering constituents [61]. Bruce Buchanan and Edward Shortliffe's edited *Rule-Based Expert Systems* (1984) records MYCIN's rule-based medical consultation and explanation mechanisms [60]. Its evaluation discussion reports approximately 75% expert approval of advice in early studies, with later blinded comparisons exposing disagreement among human experts. This was specialized medical advice, rather than broad language understanding. Expert-system inference and formal linguistic grammar are related historical tools with different purposes; neither automatically supplies the other's knowledge.

**Drawbacks and trajectory.** Ambiguity multiplies possible analyses. A grammar can license a sentence without specifying which reading is appropriate in a situation. Lexical coverage, pragmatic inference, domain knowledge, and exceptions require additional work. Hand-built pipelines amplify errors across parsing and interpretation, and expert-system knowledge acquisition becomes a maintenance problem. Statistical disambiguation and corpus-learned lexical models reduced manual coverage work; neural models later reduced task-specific feature engineering. Linguistic theory and formal semantics continued independently of that engineering shift.

**What survived.** Parse structures, semantic types, scope distinctions, executable logical forms, constrained decoding, and program-like representations survive in later systems.

**Relevance.** Compositionality explains how a small stock of primitives can support new combinations. The poverty argument motivates measuring inductive bias, but does not establish which mathematical bias is necessary. A proposed learned algebra of meaning must acquire its primitives and tolerate ambiguity without importing a hand-maintained ontology. Parsing and logical inference can themselves be superlinear or combinatorial.

## 2. Statistical NLP

**Core idea and formalism.** Estimate likely language events from observations and use probability to resolve uncertainty. An n-gram approximation is `P(w₁…wₙ) ≈ ∏ₜ P(wₜ | wₜ₋ₖ₊₁…wₜ₋₁)` for fixed order k; the noisy-channel translation objective is `argmax_e P(e)P(f|e)`.

**Landmarks.** Claude Shannon's *A Mathematical Theory of Communication* (1948) used successive statistical approximations to language [4]. Reinhard Kneser and Hermann Ney's *Improved Backing-off for M-gram Language Modeling* (1995) corrected the lower-order distribution used for unseen contexts [5]. Peter Brown, Vincent Della Pietra, Stephen Della Pietra, and Robert Mercer developed IBM translation models and parameter estimation (1993) [6]. Lawrence Rabiner's HMM tutorial (1989) consolidated methods established earlier by Baum and others [7]. John Lafferty, Andrew McCallum, and Fernando Pereira introduced CRFs (2001) [8].

Kneser-Ney uses continuation diversity: a word appearing after many distinct predecessors can be a better unseen-context prediction than a frequent word confined to a few contexts. For the interpolated bigram form, `P_KN(w|h) = max(c(hw)−D,0)/c(h) + λ(h)P_cont(w)`, with `P_cont(w) ∝ N₁⁺(·w)`. HMMs factor a joint distribution through hidden state transitions and emissions. A linear-chain CRF instead uses `P(y|x) = exp(Σₜ Σⱼ θⱼ fⱼ(yₜ₋₁,yₜ,x))/Z(x)`, allowing overlapping input features and globally normalized label sequences.

**Achievements.** Kneser and Ney reported up to about 10% lower perplexity and 5% lower word error rate in their trigram experiments [5]. IBM models learned translation probabilities and latent word alignments from bilingual corpora through EM [6]. HMM dynamic programming made speech recognition and sequence labeling tractable; CRFs addressed label bias in locally normalized conditional models and loosened observation-independence assumptions [7,8]. These are distinct tasks and their metrics should remain separate.

**Jelinek anecdote, with provenance.** Frederick Jelinek's own 2004 LREC slides reproduce “Whenever I fire a linguist our system performance improves” and attribute it to his December 1988 workshop talk [9]. The title, *Some of my Best Friends are Linguists*, and the retrospective setting matter. This verifies his later reporting of the remark, rather than a controlled comparison or a transcript of the original occasion. Versions circulating with “every time” and “speech recognizer” should be treated as paraphrases.

**Drawbacks and trajectory.** Longer n-grams fragment counts across sparse contexts and grow storage. HMM state assumptions constrain dependencies. CRFs require feature design, labeled data, and a partition function; arbitrary graph inference can become intractable. IBM translation needed bilingual data, approximate decoding, and later phrase and reordering models. Neural embeddings and recurrent models shared information across similar contexts, then neural encoder-decoders and transformers displaced phrase-based translation on major benchmarks.

**What survived.** Likelihood training, cross-entropy and perplexity, beam search, latent alignment, dynamic programming, smoothing intuition, and structured output layers. CRFs and finite-state components still suit specific labeling and speech tasks.

**Relevance.** Fixed-order n-gram scoring is linear in sequence length with bounded context. Cheap inference alone did not produce reusable abstract meanings. Kneser-Ney illustrates that a better estimator can save data and computation without simply increasing parameter count. Shannon's communication theory explicitly brackets semantic meaning; it should not be cited as a theory of ideas.

## 3. Distributional semantics

**Core idea and formalism.** Patterns of use provide evidence about lexical meaning. Compress a word-context or term-document matrix into vectors, then compare or manipulate those vectors: LSA uses `X ≈ U_k Σ_k V_kᵀ`; GloVe fits `wᵢᵀw̃ⱼ + bᵢ + b̃ⱼ ≈ log Xᵢⱼ` with frequency-dependent weights.

**Landmarks.** Zellig Harris's *Distributional Structure* (1954) analyzes environments and substitutability [10]; J. R. Firth's *A Synopsis of Linguistic Theory, 1930–1955* (1957) situates collocation within a broader account including contexts of situation [11]. Scott Deerwester, Susan Dumais, George Furnas, Thomas Landauer, and Richard Harshman's LSA paper (1990) used SVD for retrieval [12]. Tomas Mikolov, Kai Chen, Greg Corrado, and Jeffrey Dean introduced efficient CBOW and skip-gram embeddings (2013) [13]. Mikolov, Wen-tau Yih, and Geoffrey Zweig examined linguistic offsets (2013) [14]. Jeffrey Pennington, Richard Socher, and Christopher Manning introduced GloVe (2014) [15].

**Achievements.** LSA used roughly 50–100 dimensions in reported applications and retrieved documents through latent associations even when query terms were absent [12]. Word2vec demonstrated learning vectors from 1.6 billion words with much lower computational cost than earlier neural embedding methods [13]. The linguistic-regularities paper reported almost 40% accuracy on its syntactic analogy questions [14]. GloVe's 300-dimensional model trained on 42 billion tokens achieved 75.0% overall analogy accuracy in its Table 2, on a different test setup from the earlier linguistic-regularities result [15]. A familiar illustration is `v(king) − v(man) + v(woman) ≈ v(queen)`; this is a nearest-neighbor relation in particular learned spaces.

**Limits of analogy arithmetic.** Tal Linzen (2016) showed that cosine-neighborhood structure contributes to apparent offset success. In his notation `a:b :: a*:b*`, without excluding input words, the nearest neighbor of `a*−a+b` was b in 93% of tested cases and a* in 5%; reversing analogy direction reduced average accuracy by 0.11 [16]. Such results require baselines and task details. They do not prove that conceptual relations form a universal additive algebra.

**Drawbacks and trajectory.** A single static vector mixes senses. Co-occurrence brings antonyms together as well as synonyms and inherits social and corpus biases. Bag-of-words LSA loses order; local embedding objectives poorly constrain negation, binding, quantifiers, and compositional inference. Dense factorization has costs in corpus and vocabulary size. Contextual neural representations overtook static embeddings for many downstream tasks because the same word could receive different representations in different sentences.

**What survived.** Embedding layers, cosine retrieval, dense indexing, representation transfer, similarity geometry, and objectives connecting prediction with latent factors.

**Relevance.** These methods establish that useful relational geometry can emerge from linguistic observations without manually encoding every relation. They also expose a gap between similarity and meaning-preserving computation. Word2vec's billion-word training scale gives little support to a child-scale acquisition claim. A research target is a representation where composition, role reversal, and negation have testable operators rather than appealing pictures of nearby words.

## 4. Neural language models, recurrence, and the attention bridge

**Core idea and formalism.** Share learned continuous features across contexts and predict words from a state. A recurrent LM updates `hₜ = f(W_hh hₜ₋₁ + W_xh xₜ)` and predicts `P(wₜ₊₁|w₁:ₜ) = softmax(W_o hₜ)`; gates regulate which state components are retained or updated.

**Landmarks.** Yoshua Bengio, Réjean Ducharme, Pascal Vincent, and Christian Jauvin's neural probabilistic LM (2003) jointly learned word features and a finite-context probability function [17]. Tomas Mikolov, Martin Karafiát, Lukáš Burget, Jan Černocký, and Sanjeev Khudanpur's RNN LM (2010) used recurrence for speech recognition [18]. Sepp Hochreiter and Jürgen Schmidhuber's LSTM (1997) introduced controlled error flow through memory cells [19]. Kyunghyun Cho and colleagues' encoder-decoder (2014) introduced the gated unit now called GRU [20]. Ilya Sutskever, Oriol Vinyals, and Quoc Le's seq2seq system (2014) mapped source sequences to fixed-width vectors and decoded target sequences [21]. Dzmitry Bahdanau, Cho, and Bengio added learned soft alignment in a September 2014 preprint, presented at ICLR 2015 [22].

A modern LSTM cell is often written `cₜ = fₜ⊙cₜ₋₁ + iₜ⊙gₜ`. This is the familiar later forget-gate formulation, rather than a claim that every gate in present implementations appeared in the original 1997 architecture. A GRU mixes retained and candidate state with an update gate and uses a reset gate in computing the candidate. Bahdanau attention computes `αₜⱼ = softmax_j(a(sₜ₋₁,hⱼ))`, `cₜ = Σⱼ αₜⱼ hⱼ`. A decoder can consult different source states at different output steps.

**Achievements.** Bengio et al. improved on n-gram baselines on two corpora through parameter sharing. On Brown, their Table 1 reports that the best n-gram baseline's test perplexity was 24% higher than the neural model selected by validation perplexity [17]. This states the comparison's direction; it does not mean a 24% reduction from the baseline. Mikolov et al. reported around 50% perplexity reduction from an RNN mixture relative to a backoff LM and around 18% relative word-error reduction on WSJ with equal training-data amounts; their NIST RT05 comparison gave around 5% word-error reduction [18]. LSTM bridged delays exceeding 1,000 steps on artificial tasks [19]. Sutskever et al. reported 34.8 BLEU on WMT14 English-to-French versus 33.3 for the cited phrase-based system, and 36.5 when reranking its hypotheses [21]. These translation results are unrelated to the transformer's English-to-German score quoted later. Bahdanau et al. achieved performance comparable to a phrase-based English-to-French system while easing the fixed-vector bottleneck [22].

**Drawbacks and trajectory.** Products of recurrent Jacobians cause gradients to shrink or grow across steps; gated additive paths mitigate that problem. Recurrence still enforces a training dependency between consecutive states. A fixed-size encoder state compresses an entire source into a limited channel. Attention reduced that bottleneck but attending over all source positions at every target step introduced an O(n_source n_target) interaction term. Large vocabulary softmax and backpropagation also cost computation. Transformers replaced recurrence with parallelizable attention and shorter paths between token positions.

**What survived.** Autoregressive factorization, embeddings, encoder-decoder training, gates, learned state, and attention itself. Recurrent methods remain relevant when bounded state and streaming matter.

**Relevance.** Mikolov's results are direct evidence that a representation can outperform counting with less in-domain data, within a defined speech task. Fixed-width recurrence is O(nd²) for dense state transitions and has O(d) inference state per layer, excluding parameters and training activations. It cannot preserve arbitrary exact histories in finite precision. The transformer's training advantage should therefore be separated from the mathematical question of whether recurrence can yield a more data-efficient abstraction.

## 5. Compositional and structured neural models

**Core idea and formalism.** Follow a tree and combine child representations into a parent. A recursive network might use `h_parent = f(W[h_left;h_right]+b)`; a Tree-LSTM uses `c_parent = i⊙g + Σ_child f_child⊙c_child` to control memory from each branch.

**Landmarks and achievements.** Richard Socher, Alex Perelygin, Jean Wu, Jason Chuang, Christopher Manning, Andrew Ng, and Christopher Potts introduced recursive tensor models and the Stanford Sentiment Treebank (2013) [23]. Their RNTN reached 85.4% binary sentence sentiment accuracy and 45.7% fine-grained accuracy, as also reproduced in Tai et al.'s comparison [24]. Kai Sheng Tai, Socher, and Manning's Tree-LSTM (2015) reached 51.0% fine-grained and 88.0% binary accuracy with tuned GloVe vectors; its dependency version achieved Pearson correlation 0.8676 on the semantic-relatedness benchmark [24]. Trees provided useful bias on these tasks, especially where constituent interactions mattered.

**Drawbacks and trajectory.** An external parser supplies potentially incorrect structure. Sentiment supervision at phrase nodes contains more information than a sentence label, and pretrained vectors contain additional text exposure. Tree-shaped computation complicates batching and makes parent nodes wait for children. Syntax trees also omit discourse and many semantic relations. Sequence models simplified the pipeline and hardware execution; attention learned flexible cross-token interactions and eventually gained the advantages of large-scale pretraining. This is an adoption explanation, not evidence that syntactic structure is useless or that every sequence model beat every tree model.

**What survived.** Recursive composition, tree and graph message passing, structured inductive bias, and phrase-level evaluation.

**Relevance.** For a supplied tree with O(n) nodes, composition can be linear in n at fixed width. Parsing and searching over latent trees add separate costs. The relevant experiment is whether a learned structure reduces sample complexity when supervision and pretraining exposure are equal. Published sentiment accuracy alone cannot establish general language data efficiency.

## 6. Compositional distributional semantics: DisCoCat

**Core idea and formalism.** Let grammar determine how vector meanings combine through typed tensor contractions. A mapping from grammatical reductions to linear maps produces a sentence vector while preserving composition [25].

**Landmarks.** Bob Coecke, Mehrnoosh Sadrzadeh, and Stephen Clark's *Mathematical Foundations for a Compositional Distributional Model of Meaning* (2010) joins Lambek pregroup grammar with vector spaces using shared category-theoretic structure [25]. Edward Grefenstette and Sadrzadeh (2011) implemented corpus-learned relational matrices and evaluated sentence similarity/disambiguation [26].

For a transitive construction, grammatical types can reduce as `n (nʳ s nˡ) n → s`. With noun space N and sentence space S, a verb is in `N⊗S⊗N` and `sentence = (ε_N⊗1_S⊗ε_N)(subject⊗verb⊗object)`. The evaluation maps ε contract noun arguments with the verb tensor. The functorial condition `F(g∘f)=F(g)∘F(f)` expresses compatibility with composition.

**Achievements.** The 2010 work supplies a common sentence space independent of a sentence's grammatical shape and a diagrammatic description of information flow; using Boolean scalars connects to Montague-style semantics [25]. In the 2011 implementation, Spearman correlation with human judgments was 0.17 on the intransitive test, matching the compared multiplicative model, and 0.21 on the transitive test [26]. These small correlations demonstrate task-specific gains and leave substantial unexplained variation. They supply no general-purpose LM comparison.

**Drawbacks and trajectory.** A dense verb tensor has d_N²d_S entries. If all widths equal d, that is d³, and additional arguments or higher-order types increase the burden. Grammar selection, lexical sparsity, negation, scope, and interpretation remain learning problems. Low-rank factorization reduces storage while restricting the operators available. Grammar-led models faced more setup and estimation constraints than broadly trained sequence networks. The research program continued, including tensor-network formulations; it was never the dominant production language-model architecture.

**What survived.** Typed composition, tensor contractions, diagrammatic reasoning, and a specification of how a continuous meaning space can respect grammatical combination.

**Relevance.** This lineage addresses Andrew's mathematical question most directly: a sentence can be an application of learned operators to arguments. The formalism does not guarantee that its types or tensors capture human ideas or can be learned from few observations. A next step would test parameter sharing and low-rank composition against unstructured models at equal data and compute, while tracking what meaning distinctions compression destroys.

## 7. Vector-symbolic architectures and hyperdimensional computing

**Core idea and formalism.** Represent an entity and its role separately, then bind them into a distributed representation that can be composed and approximately unpacked. Tensor-product binding uses `T = Σᵢ fᵢ⊗rᵢ`; holographic binding replaces the expanding product with circular convolution, `(f⊛r)ₖ = Σⱼ fⱼr_(k−j mod d)` [27,28].

**Landmarks.** Paul Smolensky's tensor-product representation (1990) formalized distributed role-filler binding [27]. Tony Plate's holographic reduced representations, presented earlier and developed in the 1995 journal paper, encoded bindings, sequences, and recursive frames in fixed-width vectors [28]. Pentti Kanerva's *Sparse Distributed Memory* (1988) used high-dimensional distributed addressing [30]; his hyperdimensional-computing tutorial (2009) unified related operations and models [29]. The 2009 paper illustrates the geometry with 10,000-bit vectors, rather than establishing that this width is optimal for language.

**Achievements.** These works specify how distributed vectors can carry structured information rather than only an unstructured similarity score. With independent role vectors, tensor-product unbinding contracts against a role's dual. Plate demonstrated approximate reconstruction of variable bindings and frames in a fixed-width space using a separate cleanup memory. Kanerva described noisy-address recall and operations including superposition, binding, and permutation [27–30]. The primary contribution is an algebra and capacity analysis, with demonstrations of recall and structure; these sources offer no broad language benchmark showing superiority to LLMs.

**Drawbacks and trajectory.** Exact tensor products grow as products of constituent dimensions and can grow exponentially with recursive depth. Reduced representations retain fixed width by accepting interference and noisy reconstruction. Superposed items compete for capacity. Cleanup requires a stored vocabulary and nearest-neighbor retrieval, whose straightforward cost is O(Md). Similarity between random codes is not acquired semantic understanding. These architectures remained a smaller research program as gradient-trained representations gained broader task performance and software support. Their limited adoption does not demonstrate that binding is unnecessary.

**What survived.** Role-filler separation, associative memory, approximate unbinding, and high-dimensional algebra remain available for learned structured models. The same general concerns reappear in neural memory addressing. A specific claim that transformer attention directly implements Plate's HRR algebra would require additional evidence.

**Relevance.** This is a candidate for bounded-state representations of ideas and relations. Elementwise binary binding or permutation is O(d); FFT convolution is O(d log d), versus O(d²) for a naive convolution. Those costs are in representation width. Applying a fixed number of such operations per word is linear in sequence length, but decoding, cleanup, and capacity growth must be counted. A finite-precision fixed-width vector cannot preserve unlimited arbitrary information. The research question is which structured distinctions can be preserved under useful compression, and how the system learns the codes and operators.

## 8. Memory-augmented networks

**Core idea and formalism.** Separate a learned controller from an addressable memory, allowing stored data to be reused without encoding everything in the controller's weights. Differentiable reading uses `rₜ = Σᵢ aₜᵢ Mₜ[i]`; writing combines soft erasure and addition [31].

**Landmarks.** Alex Graves, Greg Wayne, and Ivo Danihelka introduced Neural Turing Machines (2014) [31]. Jason Weston, Sumit Chopra, and Antoine Bordes introduced Memory Networks (2014 preprint, ICLR 2015) for question answering [32]. Sainbayar Sukhbaatar, Arthur Szlam, Weston, and Rob Fergus's end-to-end variant (2015) removed supporting-fact supervision [33]. Graves, Wayne, Malcolm Reynolds, and colleagues introduced the Differentiable Neural Computer (2016), adding allocation and temporal-link mechanisms [34].

**Achievements.** The NTM learned copying, sorting, and associative recall. Trained on copy sequences of lengths 1–20, it copied lengths 30 and 50 with few errors; the length-120 example had both local errors and a duplicated item. The 128-location memory eventually overwrote earlier writes [31]. On associative recall, the feedforward-controller NTM approached zero loss after about 30,000 episodes, while the comparison LSTM did not reach zero after a million episodes. This is an algorithm-learning result, with substantial training experience. Memory Networks considered up to 14 million stored sentences and reported about an eightfold speedup with clustered hashing at similar QA performance [32]. End-to-end Memory Networks achieved LM performance comparable to, or slightly exceeding, similarly sized RNN/LSTM baselines on Penn Treebank and Text8, with gains from multiple hops [33]. DNC demonstrations included graph shortest paths, relational questions, and symbol-specified blocks tasks [34].

**Drawbacks and trajectory.** Controllers must learn what to write, where, when to retain it, and how to retrieve it, with gradients crossing many recurrent operations. Diffuse reads can mix unrelated values; incorrect writes corrupt later reasoning. Original Memory Networks often used supporting-fact supervision, and the end-to-end paper says smooth lookup may scale poorly [32,33]. A soft scan costs O(Md) per head per step. Exact DNC temporal linkage uses an M×M matrix. The checked author manuscript also describes a sparse approximation with O(M log M) computation and O(M) memory and reports no discernible loss on its tested tasks [34]. Quadratic linkage is a cost of the exact implementation, rather than an unavoidable property of external neural memory. The literature demonstrated selected tasks rather than an established path to open-domain language acquisition. Attention encoders and retrieval systems offered easier-to-deploy pieces of memory functionality; general writeable neural memory remained less common.

**What survived.** Content-based retrieval, multiple reasoning hops, controller-memory separation, and explicit memory management. Retrieval-augmented language systems share this separation, though most do not learn an NTM-style continuous write process end to end.

**Relevance.** Separating facts from procedures could reduce relearning and weight growth. These papers also show why putting knowledge outside the network does not automatically reduce total memory or lookup compute. One-shot storage of a new fact and one-shot acquisition of a new concept are different achievements. A candidate should show reliable retrieval and bounded access cost while learning reusable operations.

## 9. Bayesian program induction and the language of thought

**Core idea and formalism.** Explain observations with compact generative procedures, then reuse their parts for new concepts. Bayesian induction scores `P(program|D) ∝ P(D|program)P(program)`; a structured prior makes some abstractions plausible after few examples [36–40].

**Landmarks.** Jerry Fodor's *The Language of Thought* (1975) proposed combinatorial internal representations distinct from spoken language [35]. Joshua Tenenbaum's *Bayesian Modeling of Human Concept Learning* (NIPS 1998) explains generalization from few positive examples through hypotheses and a sampling model [36]. Brenden Lake, Ruslan Salakhutdinov, and Tenenbaum's Bayesian Program Learning model (2015) represents handwritten characters as probabilistic drawing programs [37]. Noah Goodman, Tenenbaum, and Tobias Gerstenberg's 2014 memo develops concepts as stochastic functions in a probabilistic language of thought [38]. Steven Piantadosi, Tenenbaum, and Goodman modeled number-word learning through program-like composition (2012) and tested competing logical primitive sets (2016) [39,40]. Goodman and Piantadosi's relevant pre-transformer work includes these shared papers; the 2014 concepts chapter has Gerstenberg as its third author.

For uniformly sampled positive examples within a hypothesis h, Tenenbaum's size principle gives `P(D|h) = |h|^(−m)` when all m observations are included in h, and zero otherwise. The assumed sampling process matters. Observations chosen deliberately by a teacher can support different inferences from arbitrary encountered examples.

**Achievements.** On 20-way within-alphabet one-shot character classification, BPL had 3.3% error, humans 4.5%, a comparison deep convnet 13.5%, and a task-optimized Siamese convnet 8.0% [37]. Removing compositionality increased BPL error to 14.0%. Learning-to-learn and causal drawing structure were also part of its advantage. With only five background alphabets, BPL achieved 4.3% and 4.0% error in two training subsets; the comparison convnet had 24.0% and 22.3%. The model additionally generated new exemplars and concepts for human judgments. These were character concepts, with priors and background experience, rather than one-shot language learning from an empty state.

The number-learning model reconstructs a developmental transition from meanings for a few numerals to a recursive numerical system [39]. The 2016 experiments distinguished primitive sets through their predicted learning curves and favored a richer set of Boolean operations including first-order quantification [40]. This gives an empirical way to test proposed primitives rather than choosing them solely for mathematical convenience.

**Drawbacks and trajectory.** Search over programs is combinatorial; even evaluating candidates can be costly or fail to terminate without restrictions. Approximate posterior search can get stuck or miss the right abstraction. The drawing model relies on stroke-like primitives and a domain-appropriate causal model. Selecting the hypothesis language determines which new concepts are easy to express. These methods remained useful in constrained induction and cognitive modeling, while dense networks offered broad, batchable training with less domain-specific setup. There is no evidence here that Bayesian induction was disproven by transformers.

**What survived.** Hierarchical priors, meta-learning, causal generative models, program synthesis, reusable libraries, and inference from examples. A neural proposal mechanism can potentially amortize search, but that is a proposed direction here, not a result established by the cited BPL comparison.

**Relevance.** This lineage provides the clearest concrete evidence for few-shot benefits from reusable abstract structure. A useful new mathematics would connect learned primitives, compositional expressivity, and the cost of finding a program. A prior that encodes the answer in advance must be charged as knowledge. A successful successor must make the search bill visible and demonstrate transfer beyond the domain for which the primitives were designed.

## 10. Connectionist and symbolic debates: systematicity

**Core idea and formalism.** Representational architecture should explain why abilities occur in related combinations. If a learner can represent `loves(John,Mary)`, the same constituents and roles should support `loves(Mary,John)`; that relation between capacities is systematicity [41].

**Landmark and contribution.** Fodor and Zenon Pylyshyn's *Connectionism and Cognitive Architecture: A Critical Analysis* (1988) argued that combinatorial syntax and semantics were necessary at the cognitive level, even if neural networks implemented them [41]. It presented an architectural argument rather than benchmark measurements. The argument distinguished learning correlations among whole patterns from having constituent-sensitive operations that support a family of related thoughts.

**What it predicted, with limits.** The debate made variable binding, productivity, role reversal, and generalization to unseen combinations explicit tests. Smolensky's binding algebra, recursive networks, and neural external memory responded with mechanisms addressing parts of that challenge [27,31]. It did not predict that every neural architecture would fail every compositional task, or establish that an implemented symbolic architecture would learn language efficiently.

**Drawbacks and trajectory.** Requiring compositional structure leaves open how primitives arise, how context affects meaning, and how noise is handled. A symbolic system can represent unlimited combinations while its search or acquisition remains impractical. Connectionist results demonstrated learned distributed structure, weakening the use of an unstructured associative net as a proxy for all neural computation. The dispute persisted as an empirical question about which architectures generalize under which distributions; it was never settled by ordinary held-out accuracy alone.

**What survived.** Tests for variable binding, compositional generalization, productivity, and out-of-distribution role combinations.

**Relevance.** Andrew's claim should be tested on novel combinations and role changes, not just word similarity or fluent reconstruction. For a learned algebra, the same operation should work on new fillers and compose across depth. Its formal closure properties and its acquired generalization should both be measured. Systematicity by itself provides no complexity bound.

## 11. Cognitive architectures and grounded language

**Core idea and formalism.** Coordinate perception, memory, goals, and actions around a model of a situation. Production systems use rules of the form `conditions → action`; a grounded interpreter relates an utterance to a state transition or a query over the represented world.

**Landmarks.** Terry Winograd's MIT report *Procedures as a Representation for Data in a Computer Program for Understanding Natural Language* (1971) describes SHRDLU; the source notes a 1970 dissertation origin [44]. John Laird, Allen Newell, and Paul Rosenbloom's Soar architecture (1987) combines problem spaces, productions, subgoals, and chunking [42]. John Anderson, Daniel Bothell, Michael Byrne, Scott Douglass, Christian Lebiere, and Yulin Qin's ACT-R synthesis (2004) combines specialized modules, buffers, symbolic rules, and subsymbolic selection [43]. ACT-R belongs to an older developmental line; 2004 is a synthesis date.

**Achievements.** SHRDLU answered questions, executed commands, resolved some discourse ambiguity, and discussed plans in a simulated blocks world. Soar cached results of subgoal reasoning into productions, allowing later similar cases to bypass search [42]. ACT-R modeled coordinated performance across perception, memory, and action and related modules to empirical cognitive and neural data [43]. These sources demonstrate integrated behavior or cognitive fits. They do not demonstrate competitive unrestricted text learning.

**Drawbacks and trajectory.** SHRDLU's objects, actions, and world knowledge were supplied within a limited microworld. Broadening the world requires new representations and knowledge. Soar and ACT-R depend on task models and rules; chunking can enlarge stored knowledge, and planning can be combinatorial. These programs remained active for cognitive modeling and agent design. Data-driven language systems overtook them in broad text coverage because their inputs and tasks were easier to scale through corpora, while the architecture programs pursued different explanatory goals.

**What survived.** Goal-conditioned reasoning, procedural versus declarative memory, chunking, and grounding language in executable consequences.

**Relevance.** Grounding and action provide information that text-only counts omit. These architectures also show the risk Andrew wants to avoid: supplied world models and rule bases can make apparent language competence depend on human engineering. Reusing learned procedures is relevant; recreating a manually populated expert-system shell would not meet his stated aim.

## 12. How much language children hear, and what the comparison establishes

**Core measurement.** Cumulative exposure is approximately `words heard = Σ_day measured or estimated word count`. Different studies count different speech: child-directed words, clearly recorded nearby adults, or overheard conversations. Exposure measures provide constraints on learning theories, with no inherited computational guarantee.

**Hart and Risley.** Their 1995 work, summarized by the authors in *The Early Catastrophe* (2003), observed 42 families for roughly an hour per month over about 2½ years [45]. They reported group averages of 616, 1,251, and 2,153 words per hour. Extrapolating to 5,200 waking hours per year and four years gave roughly **13 million, 26 million, and 45 million words by age four**. These totals are extrapolations from sampled hours, rather than full lifetime recordings. The article's title says “by age 3,” while the numerical cumulative calculation in its body explicitly uses four years. Use the calculation's stated age when quoting those totals.

**Gilkerson et al.** The 2017 paper uses 3,213 standardized 12-hour recordings from 329 monolingual English-speaking families, with a longitudinal subsample [46]. Table 5 reports a mean adult count of **12,709 words per recording day**, SD **4,274** for family-averaged daily counts. Means by maternal-education group range from **11,358 to 14,848 words per day**. Multiplying the overall mean by 365 and four gives **about 18.6 million words**, a calculation in this brief. It assumes a constant daily rate across the entire first four years, including ages outside the recordings, and ignores unrecorded hours. It is an order-of-magnitude illustration, not an observed lifetime total. The algorithm does not distinguish child-directed from overheard speech, and it filters some overlapping, distant, and unclear speech.

**Contested sampling and social interpretation.** Douglas Sperry, Linda Sperry, and Peggy Miller's study, published online in 2018 and in a 2019 issue, examined 42 children across five communities [47]. It did not support a uniform 30-million-word socioeconomic gap and argued that excluding other caregivers and bystander speech undercounts some children's environments. Gilkerson et al. likewise reported substantial within-group variability. There is no single universal human word budget. The defensible historical comparison is **tens of millions of early-childhood spoken-word exposures**, with sampling and population qualifications, versus hundreds of billions or trillions of training tokens.

**Comparison with model training.** Tom Brown and colleagues trained GPT-3 on **300 billion tokens** [55]. Hoffmann et al. trained Chinchilla on **1.4 trillion tokens** [54]. Numerically dividing these counts by Hart and Risley's 13–45 million word range gives about **6,700–23,000** for GPT-3 and **31,000–108,000** for Chinchilla. These are ratios of reported token exposures to estimated word exposures, not ratios in a common unit and not measures of equal capability. Tokenization varies by language and text, repeated training exposures count again, and children continue learning beyond age four. GPT-3's paper explicitly describes unequal repeated sampling of its datasets. Avoid presenting those ratios as an exact sample-efficiency disadvantage.

**A separate rapid-learning observation.** Jenny Saffran, Richard Aslin, and Elissa Newport (1996) found evidence that eight-month-old infants could segment an artificial speech stream using syllable transition statistics after **two minutes** of exposure [57]. This isolates a small learning problem. It neither establishes full language acquisition in two minutes nor reveals a complete algebra of concepts.

**Interpretation, limits, and what survived.** Children gain embodied, interactive, social, and multimodal experience that these word totals do not measure; evolutionary and developmental priors also matter. LLMs learn far more written factual material than a four-year-old is tested on. These observations motivate low-exposure experiments and multimodal learning, but cannot isolate architecture as the cause of the gap. What survives from the acquisition literature is the need to count the entire learning history, interactions, and input quality. The disputed socioeconomic number should remain a qualified historical estimate, rather than a premise for a new model.

## 13. Additional lineage: grammar induction, learnability, and compression

**Core idea and formalism.** Learn the grammar or procedure that gives a compact explanation of the observations, balancing fit against complexity. Minimum description length chooses `argmin_G [L(G)+L(D|G)]`; Bayesian selection uses the related tradeoff between prior and likelihood [48–50].

**Landmarks.** E. Mark Gold's *Language Identification in the Limit* (1967) studied formal learnability [48]. Jorma Rissanen's *Modeling by Shortest Data Description* (1978) established an explicit description-length model-selection criterion [49]. Andreas Stolcke and Stephen Omohundro's *Inducing Probabilistic Grammars by Bayesian Model Merging* (1994) began with observed samples and generalized by merging states or nonterminals and introducing chunks [50].

**Achievements.** Gold specified exactly what eventual identification means and showed that language classes containing all finite languages plus an infinite language cannot be identified from every arbitrary positive presentation under his criterion. In particular, the full regular-language class fails that guarantee. This result depends on the hypothesis class and presentation assumptions; it does not show that children cannot learn from natural positive input. Bayesian merging learned some finite-state and recursive structures while trading likelihood against a simpler model. Stolcke and Omohundro report mixed results on natural-language corpora and use beams of width 3–10 for their context-free search [50].

**Drawbacks and trajectory.** Search over grammars is expensive, local merges can be misleading, and a compression prior can favor the wrong analysis. The 1994 source specifically observes that encoding agreement in a CFG can enlarge a grammar and cause a simplicity bias to prefer an incorrect bracketing. Dense prediction models scaled through continuous optimization and avoided enumerating grammar changes. Grammar induction remained useful for particular formal languages and cognitive questions rather than becoming the default broad language-model pipeline.

**What survived.** Compression-based regularization, learned reusable chunks, probabilistic grammars, and explicit conditions under which generalization is possible.

**Relevance.** This is directly relevant to the idea that a small abstract representation could explain extensive language. Compact explanations must predict new structure and preserve meaning distinctions. Search cost and the choice of description language need to be exposed; a short program may be slow, and a short encoding of surface text may fail to capture causal or semantic structure.

## 14. Additional lineage: convolutional sequence models

**Core idea and formalism.** Repeated local filters share computation across positions, building larger receptive fields through depth. A layer computes `hₜ^(ℓ+1) = f(Σⱼ₌₀^(k−1) Wⱼ h_(t−j)^ℓ)`; gated units and residual paths support training.

**Landmark and achievements.** Jonas Gehring, Michael Auli, David Grangier, Denis Yarats, and Yann Dauphin's *Convolutional Sequence to Sequence Learning* appeared as a May 2017 preprint before the transformer's June preprint [51]. The ICML publication came later. They reported better WMT14 English-to-German and English-to-French accuracy than the cited deep-LSTM system and an order-of-magnitude speed improvement on GPU and CPU. Training over positions was parallelizable. The paper's use of attention at decoder layers remains part of the architecture.

**Drawbacks and trajectory.** Ordinary filters need depth for distant interactions; dilation changes that tradeoff. Receptive-field size and a task's dependence range must agree. Their attention modules still scan source positions, so the complete translation model is not purely linear-time. Transformers allowed direct content-dependent interaction between any pair of positions at each attention layer and soon improved the cited translation benchmarks [52].

**What survived.** Locality, convolutional token mixing, gating, and residual composition.

**Relevance.** Parallel training was already possible before transformers. A fixed-depth local convolution has O(nkd²) mixing cost, but its bounded receptive field restricts access to context. This is useful evidence for separating efficient mixing from learned abstraction. Removing quadratic attention must be evaluated together with the replacement's information-access limits.

## 15. Additional lineage: latent-variable topic models

**Core idea and formalism.** Explain a document as a mixture of shared latent topics, each a probability distribution over words. LDA draws `θ_doc ~ Dirichlet(α)`, then `zₜ ~ Categorical(θ_doc)` and `wₜ ~ Categorical(β_zₜ)` [56].

**Landmark and achievements.** David Blei, Andrew Ng, and Michael Jordan's *Latent Dirichlet Allocation* (2003) provides a Bayesian generative document model with approximate variational inference [56]. It evaluated document modeling, text classification, and collaborative filtering against mixture-of-unigrams and probabilistic-LSI baselines. The shared topics gave a compact probabilistic representation that could describe new documents, without storing a separate learned parameter vector for each training document as in basic pLSI.

**Drawbacks and trajectory.** Exchangeable word counts discard sentence order and role binding. A topic groups associated terms and can reflect a corpus artifact; calling it an idea does not supply a compositional semantics. Inference is iterative, and the number and interpretation of topics remain modeling choices. Embeddings and contextual encoders offered better task transfer and flexible token-level representations, reducing topic models' prominence as general NLP representations. Topic models remain useful when corpus organization and inspectable distributions are the goal.

**What survived.** Latent factors, hierarchical Bayesian sharing, mixture representations, and approximate inference.

**Relevance.** This is an early explicit attempt to learn an abstract level above word identities. It demonstrates shared latent explanations, while revealing why a bag of topics is insufficient for a relation such as who did what to whom. Token-level inference often costs at least O(nK) per naive iteration; the number of iterations and vocabulary-by-topic storage must be counted. It offers no established few-shot open-language advantage.

## 16. What Attention Is All You Need changed, and why it won

Ashish Vaswani, Noam Shazeer, Niki Parmar, Jakob Uszkoreit, Llion Jones, Aidan Gomez, Łukasz Kaiser, and Illia Polosukhin introduced the Transformer (2017) [52]. Its defining move was to use attention for sequence transduction without recurrent or convolutional sequence layers. Learned projections and multiple heads compute

`Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(QKᵀ/√d_k)V`.

Positional representations supply sequence order. Residual connections, normalization, and feed-forward layers remain essential parts of the model; “based solely on attention” in the paper describes its replacement of sequence-transduction mechanisms, rather than the absence of other operations.

Training with known target sequences permits position-parallel computation under a causal mask. Dense self-attention also offers a short path between distant positions without first compressing the prefix into a single recurrent state. The paper reports **28.4 BLEU** on WMT14 English-to-German and **41.8 BLEU** on English-to-French in its abstract and Table 2, with eight GPUs used in its reported training setup. The accessible arXiv version's English-to-French discussion also contains **41.0**, conflicting with the abstract/table [52]. This brief uses the explicitly labeled abstract/table score and records that discrepancy. Training parallelism, translation quality, and convenient dense matrix operations explain the early engineering advantage; later language-model scaling supplied broader adoption evidence.

Attention was already present in Bahdanau's encoder-decoder and in neural memory. Learned hidden features also predate transformers: David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton, and Ronald Williams's backpropagation paper (1986) demonstrated feature acquisition [58], and Jeffrey Elman's *Finding Structure in Time* (1990) showed context-sensitive recurrent representations and emergent lexical categories [59]. Transformer success therefore does not establish that its token-to-token mixing is the only route to abstract representation.

**Cost and scope.** Full dense attention forms n² pair interactions and costs O(n²d) for the attention products. Projection and feed-forward terms add roughly O(nd²). A straightforward implementation stores O(n²) scores per head; materializing all scores is implementation-dependent, while evaluating all pairs remains a quadratic arithmetic cost. Standard autoregressive decoding is still sequential. With a KV cache, one new token attends over its prefix, with O(nd) attention work per step and O(nd) cache storage per layer, apart from parameters; generating an n-token sequence produces quadratic total attention work. Thus the training parallelism advantage coexists with context-dependent inference memory and compute. Efficient recurrence, local mixing, and sparse access remain relevant alternatives to evaluate.

## 17. Scaling laws: the evidence for large data requirements

**Kaplan et al., 2020.** Jared Kaplan, Sam McCandlish, Tom Henighan, Tom Brown, and colleagues found approximate power laws relating LM cross-entropy to model size, dataset size, and compute over measured regimes [53]. A useful schematic is `L(N) ≈ (N₀/N)^α_N` or `L(D) ≈ (D₀/D)^α_D` when the other resources are sufficiently large; these are empirical fits. Their compute-allocation conclusion favored larger models trained short of convergence and found larger models more sample-efficient within the studied family. Increasing size can improve loss per observed token while increasing total resource use.

**Hoffmann et al., 2022.** Jordan Hoffmann, Sebastian Borgeaud, Arthur Mensch, and colleagues trained more than 400 models, with 70 million to over 16 billion parameters and 5–500 billion training tokens, to study compute allocation [54]. They found model size and token count should grow in approximately equal proportion along their estimated compute-optimal frontier. Their test model, **70-billion-parameter Chinchilla**, trained on **1.4 trillion tokens**, outperformed **280-billion-parameter Gopher** at the same reported training-compute budget and achieved **67.5% MMLU accuracy**. This reduced inference cost through a smaller model and increased training-data use. For those models, the frequently quoted approximation of around 20 training tokens per parameter is a fitted operating point, not a universal requirement.

A common fitted form is `L(N,D)=E+A/N^α+B/D^β`, with a dense-training approximation `C ≈ 6ND` for FLOPs. It abstracts away context-length attention, architecture details, and hardware effects. Dataset quality, repetition, objective, and deployment constraints can shift the allocation.

**Interpretation.** These papers quantify the data and compute tradeoff of their transformer families and budgets. They provide empirical evidence of large token consumption and predictable returns to scale. They do not prove that any competent language learner must consume trillions of tokens, or that parameter count must grow indefinitely. Chinchilla specifically shows that more training data can permit fewer deployed parameters at improved measured performance. A proposal to beat LLMs must compare loss and transfer at matched exposure and compute, rather than citing the power laws as an impossibility theorem for all other representations.

## 18. Lessons for a mathematical successor

The historical evidence points to three separable research targets. First, learned compositional operators should preserve roles and support new combinations; DisCoCat, tensor-product binding, and systematicity provide formalisms and tests. Second, reusable causal or procedural parts should reduce examples needed for a new concept; BPL supplies measured evidence in a limited visual domain. Third, information access should have a controlled cost; recurrence and hyperdimensional operations offer bounded-width computation, while the memory-network literature shows how lookup and linkage can reintroduce quadratic storage or growing work.

No cited result demonstrates that natural language already contains a unique mathematical basis for all ideas. Distributional geometry is compatible with useful regularities, and compositional formalisms specify candidate structure. Multiple spaces and operator bases can explain the same observations. Grounding, inference behavior, and intervention tests are needed to distinguish them.

A comparison should count all prior data and supplied structure: pretrained embeddings, background alphabets, parse labels, hand-defined primitives, programs used to generate training examples, and any retrieval corpus. Measure novel-combination transfer, negation and quantification, role swaps, causal predictions, and few-example concept acquisition. Also record training FLOPs, search expansions, inference FLOPs, state bytes, latency, and how each changes with context length and knowledge size. Those measurements would establish whether a representation saves resources rather than moving costs into a prior, parser, search procedure, or external store.

| --- | --- | --- |
| Typed semantic composition | Sentence meanings from learned lexical operators [25,26] | Estimating high-order tensors and discovering types |
| Distributed role binding | Defined binding and approximate unbinding algebra [27–30] | Interference, cleanup, and learned semantic codes |
| Probabilistic reusable programs | 3.3% error in one-shot character classification [37] | Domain priors and combinatorial search |

| Compression-guided structure induction | Finite-state and recursive grammar induction [50] | Search and representation-dependent simplicity bias |
